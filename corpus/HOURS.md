# Hours of Work, Breaks & Scheduling

This policy applies to Westline staff. It answers the everyday questions — how long is a working
day, when does lunch happen, how early can a shift start, how much rest is there between two of
them — that the rest of the handbook assumes you already know.

Section 6 is the exception to the staff-only rule. Crew calls, meal windows and turnaround bind
everyone on a Westline call sheet, including contractors, because a tired crew is a safety problem
regardless of how the person on it is engaged. See `HANDBOOK` §2.

Where a provincial employment standard is more generous than a rule here, the provincial standard
wins. Tell People & Culture when you find one, so the text can be corrected rather than worked
around.

## 1. The standard week

### 1.1 Full-time hours

Full-time staff work **37.5 paid hours per week**: five days of **7.5 paid hours**, each with an
unpaid 30-minute meal break, which makes the ordinary working day an **eight-hour span**. The
37.5-hour week is the figure everything else is measured against — part-time pro-rating in
`PTO` §1.2, benefits eligibility in `BENEFITS` §2, and one day of paid time off, which is 7.5
hours.

The work week runs Monday 00:00 to Sunday 23:59 local. That boundary is what the weekly overtime
threshold in §7.2, the consecutive-day limit in §4.3 and the timesheet in §8.1 all count against.

### 1.2 The reference working day

**09:00–17:00** in your home market is Westline's reference working day. It is the window
`REMOTE` §6.1 uses when it asks staff working from another time zone to hold a four-hour overlap,
and it is what a colleague assumes when they book a meeting without asking.

It is a reference, not a requirement. Newsroom roles are scheduled around a rundown rather than a
clock, and a producer on the early shift has not left work at lunchtime, they have finished.

### 1.3 Core hours

**10:00–15:00** local are core hours. Inside them you are reachable and available for scheduled
collaboration; outside them you arrange your own day with your lead, provided your scheduled hours
are worked and any published shift, call sheet or rundown is met.

Core hours do not apply on a day you are on approved time off, on a published shift that falls
outside them, or where your lead has agreed a different pattern in writing.

### 1.4 Flexible and compressed weeks

Shifting your day inside the scheduling window in §2.1 — 07:30 to 15:30, say — needs your lead's
agreement and nothing more. A **compressed week** (four days of 9.375 hours, or nine days across
two weeks) needs your lead and People & Culture, is agreed for a stated period, and does not change
your weekly hours, your entitlement or any limit in §4. Compressed days are still subject to the
maximum day in §4.2 and the turnaround in §4.1.

### 1.5 Part-time and variable-hours staff

Part-time staff have a scheduled weekly hours figure recorded in the people directory, and every
figure in this policy scales to it: breaks by shift length, turnaround, maximum day and
consecutive days apply unchanged.

The **20-hour** threshold matters twice and in the same way both times: staff regularly scheduled
for at least 20 hours a week accrue paid time off (`PTO` §1.2) and are eligible for group benefits
(`BENEFITS` §2). Staff below it receive statutory vacation pay instead of an accrued bank. Nothing
in §3 or §4 changes below 20 hours — breaks and rest are not a benefit of tenure.

### 1.6 Salaried and hourly staff

Both work the 37.5-hour week. The difference is what happens past it: hourly staff are paid for
recorded hours with the overtime premium in §7.2, salaried staff bank lieu time under §7.3. Breaks,
turnaround, the maximum day and the consecutive-day limit are identical for both, and a salary is
not consent to any of them being ignored.

## 2. When hours may be worked

### 2.1 The scheduling window

Ordinary shifts are scheduled between **05:00 and 24:00** local. Any schedule inside that window
needs no approval beyond publication under §5.1.

### 2.2 Shift patterns

| Pattern | Hours | Where it is used |
|---|---|---|
| Early | 05:00–13:00 | Morning shows, breakfast radio, early digital desk |
| Day | 09:00–17:00 | Most non-broadcast roles, sales, digital, finance, People & Culture |
| Late | 14:30–22:30 | Evening bulletin, live events, late digital desk |
| Overnight desk | 22:00–06:00 | Calgary national desk only, on a four-on four-off rotation |

Weekend shifts sit inside these patterns and are published in the same schedule. A Saturday shift
is an ordinary scheduled day for a shift role, not overtime, and is not paid at a premium simply
for being a Saturday.

### 2.3 Overnight and outside-window work

Work scheduled to begin before 05:00 or to continue past midnight, other than the overnight desk
rotation in §2.2, is approved by a market director and recorded in the scheduling system. It
carries the longer 11-hour turnaround in §4.1. Where it is field work, `SAFETY` §3.1 restricts solo
night assignments outside city centres and `SAFETY` §4.4 sets what you do on the ground after
22:00.

### 2.4 Statutory holidays

Statutory holidays follow the province you are employed in, not the market you are covering, and
the provincial table is `PTO` §5.1. Working one is scheduled in advance like any other shift, and
whether it pays a premium or grants a substitute day is agreed with your lead **before** the shift
under `PTO` §5.1, not settled afterwards.

### 2.5 Outside your scheduled hours

You are not expected to read or answer messages outside your scheduled hours. There are three
exceptions and they are the whole list: you are on standby under §5.4, there is a declared
breaking-news call-out under §5.3, or you are on a live show currently on air.

Sending a message outside someone's hours is fine — people write when they have the time. Expecting
an answer is not. A lead who treats non-response outside scheduled hours as a performance matter is
raising a `CONDUCT` §5 concern about themselves, not about you.

## 3. Breaks

### 3.1 The meal break

**30 minutes, unpaid, taken no later than the end of the fifth consecutive hour of work.** It is
not counted in the 7.5 paid hours, which is why the ordinary day spans eight. You are free to leave
the building, and you are not expected to monitor messages during it.

A shift of five hours or less does not include a meal break. A shift scheduled beyond ten hours
includes a second one; on a shoot day both windows are set by the call sheet under §6.2.

### 3.2 Rest breaks

Shifts longer than six hours include **two paid 15-minute rest breaks**, one either side of the
meal break where the day allows. Rest breaks are not accumulated into a longer meal break, not
traded for an early finish, and not converted into pay.

### 3.3 When a break cannot be taken

Live coverage and breaking news sometimes make a break impossible. When that happens the 30 minutes
are **paid** and recorded on the timesheet as `meal not taken`, and the break is taken later in the
day if the day allows. It is not banked past the end of the shift and it never becomes lieu time.

A missed meal break is a scheduling failure, not an individual's problem to absorb. Three in a
month on the same rota goes to People & Culture as a staffing question.

### 3.4 Breaks in the field

Crew on assignment break at the meal window on the call sheet (§6.2). A solo shooter takes the same
breaks, and taking one does not suspend the check-in protocol in `SAFETY` §3.2 — tell the desk you
are stopping and where you are.

## 4. Rest between shifts, and how long a day can be

### 4.1 Minimum turnaround

**10 hours** between the end of one shift and the start of the next. **11 hours** where the earlier
shift ended after 23:00, was an overnight under §2.3, or was night field coverage under `SAFETY`
§4.4.

Turnaround is not yours to waive. A market director may shorten it to 9 hours for a declared major
news event; that decision is recorded, and the following turnaround is 11 hours.

### 4.2 Maximum day

An ordinary scheduled day is no longer than **10 hours** of paid time. A shoot day may be called
longer where the assignment desk approves the call sheet in advance, to a maximum of **12 hours**
(§6.1). Running past the scheduled end — past ten on an ordinary day, past twelve on a called shoot
day — needs assignment-desk approval while the day is happening, not afterwards.

**14 hours** from the start of your shift, including travel back from an assignment, is a hard
stop. At 14 hours you finish, whatever the story is doing. A lead who asks you to continue past it
is in breach of this policy, and fatigue on an assignment is reportable under `SAFETY` §7.1 like
any other hazard.

### 4.3 Consecutive days

No more than **six consecutive scheduled days**, and at least **32 consecutive hours free of work**
in each week — for rotating shift patterns, two such periods in any 14 days. A schedule that
breaches either limit is rejected by the scheduling system rather than approved and fixed later.

### 4.4 Fatigue

Fatigue is a safety condition, not a preference. Declining to drive, to continue an assignment or
to take a live position when you are too tired to do it safely is always an acceptable outcome and
is never held against you. That is the same rule the field safety policy opens with.

## 5. Schedules

### 5.1 Publication

Newsroom leads publish shift schedules **14 calendar days ahead** in the scheduling system. The
in-office days set by `REMOTE` §1.2 are published a month ahead and are not the same thing as a
shift schedule: an in-office day says where you work, a published shift says when.

### 5.2 Changing a published shift

Inside **seven days** of the shift, a change needs your agreement. Outside seven days, your lead
notifies you, with a minimum of 48 hours. A shift cancelled with less than **24 hours** notice is
paid at the three-hour minimum in §5.3.

### 5.3 Call-in and breaking news

Being called in outside your published schedule pays a minimum of **three hours**, as pay or as
lieu time under §7.3, whether or not three hours are worked. Turnaround under §4.1 runs from when
the call-in ends, so a 23:00 call-out moves the next morning's shift later. The desk moves the
shift; you do not lose the hours, and the moved shift is not counted as lateness.

### 5.4 Standby

A standby block is **12 hours** during which you are reachable within 30 minutes and fit to work.
Standby is scheduled and published like a shift, is paid at the standby allowance of **$75 per
block**, and is capped at **four blocks per person per month**. Being called out from standby is a
call-in under §5.3 and pays in addition to the allowance.

### 5.5 Shift swaps

Staff in the same market and role may swap shifts with their lead's confirmation in the scheduling
system at least **48 hours** ahead. The limits in §4 apply to the shape the swap creates, not to
the shape it started from: a swap producing seven consecutive days or an eight-hour turnaround is
refused, however willing both people are.

## 6. Crew calls, turnaround and meals in the field

### 6.1 The call sheet is the schedule

For a shoot day the call sheet is the schedule of record: call time, meal window, expected wrap and
travel. It is issued by the assignment desk before the day, and the times in this section are
Westline's standard for **everyone on a Westline call sheet, staff or contractor**.

Contractors are engaged under their own agreements and set their own working patterns. This section
is not an instruction on how to run your business; it is the shape of the day Westline books, and a
producer may not book one outside it.

### 6.2 Meal windows on a shoot day

The first meal window opens no later than **five hours** after call. A day scheduled beyond ten
hours carries a second meal window. Where the shoot genuinely cannot break, the meal is paid for
staff (§3.3) and claimable by contractors under `EXPENSE` §6.1, and the assignment desk records it.

### 6.3 Turnaround for crews

**10 hours** from wrap to the next call, **11 hours** where wrap was later than 23:00. This applies
across consecutive Westline call sheets whoever is on them: a producer may not book a call time
that breaches it, and an invoice does not make a nine-hour turnaround acceptable.

### 6.4 Travel on a shoot day

Travel from base to location and back counts inside the day for the turnaround in §6.3 and the
14-hour stop in §4.2, whether that travel is paid time for staff (§8.2) or invoiced by a contractor
(`EXPENSE` §6.1). Paid time and reimbursable expense are different questions with different
answers; this section is about the clock, not the claim.

## 7. Overtime and time in lieu

### 7.1 Authorisation

Overtime is authorised by your newsroom lead **before** it is worked. The single exception is a
breaking-news call-out under §5.3, where authorisation is recorded within 24 hours afterwards.
Unauthorised overtime is still paid — the law requires it — and is still a conversation about
scheduling.

### 7.2 When overtime starts

The statutory thresholds in our markets are **8 hours a day and 44 hours a week**. Because the
Westline standard day is 7.5 hours, the first 30 minutes past a standard day are paid at straight
time and the premium starts after that. Beyond the threshold, overtime is paid at **1.5×**.

### 7.3 Lieu time

Salaried staff bank authorised overtime as lieu time at **1.5×**, capped at **five days** banked at
any time, and use it within **90 days** of the day it was earned. Lieu time is not paid time off:
it does not enter the `PTO` bank, it is not subject to the carryover rule in `PTO` §2.1, and it is
not paid out when employment ends. Taking it is booked with your lead like any other day away.

### 7.4 Part-time staff

Part-time staff reaching the daily or weekly statutory threshold are paid the same premium. Hours
worked above a part-time schedule but below the threshold are paid at straight time, and repeated
additional hours are a reason to revisit the scheduled figure in §1.5, not a way to work full time
without the entitlements that come with it.

## 8. Recording your hours

### 8.1 Timesheets

Hourly staff submit a weekly timesheet by **Monday 12:00** for the week ending the previous Sunday.
Salaried staff record exceptions only: overtime, lieu earned or taken, standby, call-ins, and any
`meal not taken` under §3.3.

### 8.2 What counts as work time

Work time includes assignment travel beyond your base, time waiting to go live, mandatory training
and required meetings and events. It does not include the commute between home and your base, or
voluntary attendance at an event you were not scheduled for.

A reimbursable expense is not the same thing as paid time. Mileage to an assignment is claimable
under `EXPENSE` §3.4 whether or not the travel counted as work time, and the two are recorded
separately.

### 8.3 Corrections

A timesheet correction is submitted within **30 days** of the period it covers and needs your
lead's approval. Corrections older than 30 days go to People & Culture with payroll.

## 9. How this policy interacts with others

| Question | Where it lands |
|---|---|
| How many hours is one day of paid time off? | 7.5 hours, at the standard week in §1.1; the bank itself is `PTO` §1 |
| Which days am I in the office? | `REMOTE` §1.2, published a month ahead; §5.1 here is the shift schedule, not that |
| I am working from another time zone | `REMOTE` §6.1 overlap, measured against the reference day in §1.2 |
| I am returning from a medical leave on reduced hours | The graduated return schedule in `LEAVE` §4.4 replaces §1.1 for its stated period |
| I am too tired to drive home after a shoot | §4.4 and `SAFETY` §7.1 |
| My lead keeps scheduling nine-hour turnarounds | §4.1, and `CONDUCT` §5 if raising it does not fix it |
| I am a contractor asking when the crew breaks | §6, which is the part of this policy that binds you |
| I am a creator partner asking about my hours | This policy does not apply. You set your own; `CREATOR` §5 covers availability |

## 10. Worked examples

### 10.1 A late shift followed by an early one

A Winnipeg reporter finishes the late shift at 22:30 and is on the schedule for a 05:00 early the
next morning. That is a six-and-a-half-hour turnaround and §4.1 requires ten. The schedule is
wrong and the scheduling system should not have accepted it. The fix is the desk moving the early
shift to 08:30 or covering it with someone else — not the reporter agreeing to be fine with it,
which they cannot do under §4.1.

### 10.2 Called out at 21:00 on a scheduled day off

A Calgary producer is called in for breaking news at 21:00 and works until 23:45. The call-in pays
the three-hour minimum under §5.3 even though under three hours were worked, and the producer can
take it as lieu instead under §7.3. Turnaround runs from 23:45, so the 09:00 shift the next morning
moves to 10:45 at the earliest — 11 hours, because the work ended after 23:00. The moved start is
the desk's problem to cover, and the producer is not short any hours for it.

### 10.3 A twelve-hour shoot day

A Vancouver crew — a staff producer and a contract shooter — has a 07:00 call and a wrap forecast at
19:00. The first meal window opens no later than 12:00 under §6.2, and the twelve-hour called day
means a second one. Twelve hours is the most the desk may call in advance under §4.2, so running
past 19:00 needs the desk again on the day, and the 14-hour hard stop lands at 21:00 including
travel back to base. Both people are covered: §6 binds the contractor as much as the producer, and
the shooter's next Westline call cannot be before 05:00 the following morning — ten hours after the
forecast wrap, and later still if the day runs on.

### 10.4 A part-time schedule that keeps growing

An Edmonton digital producer is scheduled 18 hours a week and has worked 26 for two months. Nothing
about the extra hours is overtime — none of it passes 8 in a day or 44 in a week — so it is paid at
straight time under §7.4. What has actually happened is that the scheduled figure in §1.5 is wrong,
and at a real 26 hours the producer is above the 20-hour threshold for paid-time-off accrual
(`PTO` §1.2) and group benefits (`BENEFITS` §2). That is a conversation with People & Culture, not
a timesheet question.

### 10.5 A message at 22:40

A newsroom lead sends a note at 22:40 to a staff member whose shift ended at 17:00. Sending it is
fine. The staff member is not expected to read it, is not on standby, and there is no declared
call-out, so §2.5 says the answer waits until 09:00. If it genuinely could not wait, the mechanism
is a call-out under §5.3, which has a cost attached precisely so that the choice is deliberate.

## 11. Questions we are asked often

**How many hours is a full day?** 7.5 paid hours across an eight-hour span, with 30 minutes unpaid
for a meal. §1.1.

**Is my lunch paid?** No, and that is why the span is eight hours. It is paid only when the day
made it impossible to take, and then it is recorded. §3.1, §3.3.

**How early can a shift start?** 05:00 as an ordinary shift. Earlier than that is an overnight
under §2.3, needs a market director and carries an 11-hour turnaround.

**Can I work four longer days instead of five?** With your lead and People & Culture, for a stated
period, at the same 37.5 hours and inside the limits in §4. §1.4.

**Do I have to answer messages in the evening?** No, unless you are on standby, on a declared
call-out, or on air. §2.5.

**How much notice do I get of my shifts?** Fourteen days. Changes inside seven days of the shift
need your agreement. §5.1, §5.2.

**Does a Saturday pay more?** Not for being a Saturday. It pays more when it is overtime under
§7.2 or a statutory holiday under `PTO` §5.1.

**Can I swap a shift with a colleague?** Yes, same market and role, confirmed in the system 48
hours ahead, provided the swap does not create a seven-day run or a short turnaround. §5.5.

**I am a contractor. Does any of this apply to me?** Section 6 does, on any day you are on a
Westline call sheet. The rest does not; you invoice for days worked and set your own patterns.
`HANDBOOK` §2.
