# Respectful Workplace & Conduct

This document binds staff and contractors in full, and creator partners in the sections named in
`HANDBOOK` §2 — §2, §4, §5 and §7. It applies wherever Westline work happens: newsrooms, offices,
studios, field locations, vehicles, industry events, work travel, and every channel we communicate on,
including private messages between colleagues about work.

## 1. What this policy is for

Newsrooms run on trust and on speed. Both fail when someone is being harassed, when a complaint is
buried, or when raising a concern is more dangerous than staying quiet. This policy exists to make
raising a concern the safe and normal thing to do, and to make what happens next predictable.

## 2. The standard

Everyone at Westline is entitled to work free from harassment, discrimination, bullying and violence.
Everyone is responsible for the environment they create.

### 2.1 Discrimination

Adverse treatment on the basis of a protected ground — race, colour, ancestry, place of origin,
religion, marital or family status, physical or mental disability, sex, sexual orientation, gender
identity or expression, age, source of income, or a criminal record unrelated to the role — is
prohibited, in hiring, assignment, promotion, discipline and day-to-day treatment.

### 2.2 Harassment

Harassment is conduct a reasonable person would know to be unwelcome, which humiliates, intimidates or
degrades. It is not defined by intent, and one serious incident is enough. Repeated conduct that is
individually minor also counts.

### 2.3 Sexual harassment

Unwelcome sexual comments, advances, contact, images or requests, and any suggestion that work,
assignment or advancement depends on a sexual or romantic response.

### 2.4 Bullying

Persistent conduct that undermines a person's dignity or ability to do their job: public humiliation,
sabotage of work, exclusion from information needed to do the job, or shouting.

### 2.5 Violence

Physical force, threats, and the use of an object as a weapon, whether the aggressor is a colleague or
a member of the public. Violence from the public in the field is additionally a `SAFETY` §7 incident
and is reported on both routes.

### 2.6 What this is not

Legitimate direction is not harassment: assigning work, setting standards, giving critical feedback,
correcting errors, managing attendance and running a disciplinary process are normal management
functions. A negative performance conversation is not harassment because it was unwelcome. Editorial
disagreement, conducted with respect, is the job.

## 3. Relationships and power

Close personal relationships between a person and someone they manage, assign, evaluate or pay create
a conflict that the more senior person is responsible for declaring to a People & Culture partner.
Declaring is not a disciplinary matter; concealing is. The normal resolution is a change to the
reporting or assignment line, not to anyone's employment.

For creator partners the equivalent is a relationship with a commissioning producer, declared to
Creator Partnerships.

## 4. Public-facing conduct

### 4.1 On-camera and on-air

People who appear on air represent Westline whether or not they are working. Conduct in public that
would breach §2 breaches this policy even off shift, where it is connected to Westline by your
identification with the brand.

### 4.2 Interactions with the public

Members of the public are treated with the same standard as colleagues, including people who are
hostile to you. Do not respond to abuse with abuse. Disengage, record what happened, and report it
under §5.4 or `SAFETY` §7.

### 4.3 Online harassment directed at you

Harassment of Westline people by the public is Westline's problem, not yours alone. Report it to your
lead and to the Editorial Standards desk; do not engage. Westline will support account-level action,
police reporting and, where warranted, a temporary reassignment away from the story. `SOCIAL` §7
covers the mechanics. Creator partners report through Creator Partnerships and get the same support.

### 4.4 Confidentiality of colleagues' information

Do not disclose a colleague's personal information — their health, their leave, their pay, their
address, their immigration status, their family circumstances — to anyone without a business need.
Benefits and medical information carry a specific restriction in `BENEFITS` §7: a manager can see a
report's paid-time-off balance, and cannot see anything about their benefits.

## 5. Raising a concern

### 5.1 Where to take it

Take it to any of: your newsroom lead; your market director; **any** People & Culture partner (you do
not have to use the one assigned to your market); the Editorial Standards desk where it concerns
editorial pressure; Creator Partnerships if you are a creator partner.

Go directly to a People & Culture partner, skipping your lead, if the concern is **about** your lead,
if it involves harassment, discrimination or violence, or if you believe going through your lead would
expose you to retaliation. `HANDBOOK` §5.2 says the same thing. You do not need permission to skip a
step and you do not have to explain why you skipped it.

### 5.2 What happens next

1. **Acknowledgement within 2 business days**, naming who will handle it.
2. **An initial conversation** to understand what you want. Not every concern becomes an
   investigation; some are resolved by a conversation, a change to an assignment, or a team-level
   intervention. You are asked what outcome you are looking for, and told plainly if it is not
   available.
3. **Investigation**, if warranted — see §5.3.
4. **Outcome**, communicated to you: whether the concern was substantiated, and what action follows.
   You are told that action was taken. You are not told the specifics of another person's discipline.

### 5.3 Investigations

Investigations are conducted by a People & Culture partner with no reporting relationship to anyone
involved, or by an external investigator where the matter involves a market director or above, or
where the complaint is against People & Culture itself.

Both the person raising the concern and the person it concerns are told the allegations, are given a
chance to respond, may bring a support person, and are told the outcome. Target completion is **30
calendar days**; complex matters take longer and you are told when the estimate changes.

### 5.4 Emergencies

If anyone is in immediate danger, call emergency services first. Then tell the Field Safety desk
(`SAFETY` §7.1, four-hour clock) and a People & Culture partner. Do not wait for a process.

## 6. Confidentiality, and its limits

### 6.1 What we protect

Concerns are handled on a need-to-know basis. Details are not discussed with the wider team, and
records are held separately from personnel files.

### 6.2 What we cannot promise

We cannot promise that a concern will be handled without the person it concerns knowing about it,
because they have a right to respond to allegations against them. We will tell you that before you
decide how to proceed, and we will tell you what a given course of action will involve.

Confidentiality is also limited where there is a risk of harm to someone, where the law requires
disclosure, and where a regulator or court requires it.

### 6.3 Anonymous reports

Anonymous reports are accepted and acted on as far as they can be. An anonymous report that cannot be
corroborated may not be able to progress to an investigation, and no one can be told an outcome.

## 7. Escalation, retaliation and bad faith

### 7.1 Escalation path

Lead → market director → People & Culture partner → People & Culture director. Concerns about a
People & Culture director go to the CEO's office and are handled by an external investigator. At any
point you may skip to a People & Culture partner under §5.1.

Sensitive matters are escalated to a **People & Culture partner** rather than resolved in the
newsroom. An assistant, a lead or a colleague asked to look into a harassment complaint should not
investigate it; they should route it. This applies to automated and assistive tools as much as to
people: gathering the process and handing it to a People & Culture partner is the correct action, and
attempting to assess or adjudicate the complaint is not.

### 7.2 Retaliation

Retaliation against anyone who raises a concern in good faith, or who participates in an
investigation, is a serious breach of this policy and is treated as its own matter regardless of
whether the original concern was substantiated. Retaliation includes reassignment away from good work,
exclusion, and a sudden change in the tone of supervision.

### 7.3 Bad faith

A complaint made in bad faith — knowingly false, made to harm someone — is a breach of this policy. A
complaint that is not substantiated is **not** a bad-faith complaint, and the difference matters:
most unsubstantiated complaints are made in good faith by someone who experienced something real.

## 8. Consequences

Breach is addressed proportionately: a conversation, training, a written warning, a change of
assignment or reporting line, suspension, or termination. Serious breaches — violence, sexual
harassment, retaliation — may result in immediate termination for staff, immediate termination of a
services contract for contractors, or immediate termination of a Creator Partner Agreement under
`CREATOR` §8.2.

## 9. What is and is not a breach

The line between firm management and a breach of §2 is the question People & Culture is asked most
often. These examples are calibration, not an exhaustive list.

### 9.1 Not a breach

A lead tells a producer their package was inaccurate and has to be redone before air. Direct, and
correct: §2.6 protects legitimate direction and correcting errors is the job.

A market director declines a time-off request that collides with a blackout window under `PTO` §4.1.
Applying a published policy consistently is not adverse treatment.

An editor argues forcefully in a story meeting that a line of enquiry is weak, and is overruled.
Editorial disagreement conducted with respect is the work.

A lead documents lateness and starts a performance conversation. Managing attendance is a normal
management function (§2.6). It becomes a breach only if it is applied to one person on a protected
ground while others are not held to it — which is §2.1, and is about the pattern, not the
conversation.

### 9.2 A breach

A lead reviews the same producer's work in front of the newsroom, repeatedly, in terms about the
person rather than the work. Public humiliation is bullying under §2.4 regardless of whether the
underlying criticism is fair.

A senior producer comments on a colleague's appearance on camera in a way the colleague has asked
them to stop. §2.2 turns on whether a reasonable person would know it is unwelcome, and being asked
to stop settles that question.

A colleague repeatedly excludes someone from the information they need to do their job after that
person raised a concern. That is bullying under §2.4 and, because it followed a concern, retaliation
under §7.2 — a separate breach that stands whether or not the original concern was substantiated.

A lead tells a team member that a good assignment depends on going for drinks alone with them. §2.3,
and it does not require anything further to have happened.

Someone forwards a colleague's medical note to the wider team to explain an absence. §4.4, and where
it touches benefits information, `BENEFITS` §7.

### 9.3 The grey area, and how it is resolved

Most real cases are less clear than either list. A lead who is short with everyone under deadline is
not harassing anyone; the same lead who is short with one person, and that person is the only one on
the team with a protected characteristic, may be. The test in §2.2 is what a reasonable person would
know, and the resolution is usually a conversation rather than an investigation (§5.2, step 2).

Raise it anyway. Raising something that turns out not to be a breach is not a bad-faith complaint
(§7.3), and most unsubstantiated concerns are made in good faith by someone who experienced something
real.

## 10. If you are not sure what to do

Tell a People & Culture partner what happened and ask what your options are. That conversation does
not start a process, and you can have it without deciding anything.

Two things are worth knowing before you decide. Confidentiality has real limits (§6.2): we cannot
promise the person concerned will not learn of an allegation, because they have a right to respond.
And there is no deadline that forces you to act today — but the earlier a concern is raised, the more
of it can still be established.

For anyone acting on someone else's behalf, including an assistant or an automated tool: the correct
action is to route the concern to a People & Culture partner with the process explained, and to offer
to raise it. Assessing whether the described conduct amounts to harassment, weighing the account, or
advising on whether it is worth pursuing is not the correct action, is outside anyone's role but the
investigator's, and can compromise the process before it starts (§7.1).
